## No brief

Client work gives you a problem. It also gives you somewhere to put the blame when a piece is boring — the client wanted it that way, the date had to be that big, the sponsor had to be on the front.

This series has none of that. No client, no deadline, no information that has to be legible from a distance. Just a rectangle and a decision about what belongs in it.

That is a harder brief than it sounds, and it is where the typography gets honest. When nothing is required, everything is a choice you have to defend.

## One idea per poster

The rule I hold myself to: one poster, one idea.

*DO YOU NOW* is a single sentence cut into three sizes and dropped onto a sky. *MOREWARD* is a grid of crops with one enormous word wedged sideways into it. *PASTURES* is a landscape with the word set across the top of the hill, and nothing else competing for the eye.

A composition can carry one strong move — a scale jump, a collision, a colour that does not belong — and it will read as deliberate. Two moves and it reads as noise. Most of my discarded versions are not badly drawn; they are two posters fighting inside one frame.

## Series, not collection

These are made as a set, which changes what each one is allowed to do.

Individually, each poster only has to work on its own terms. As a series, they also have to share something — a proportion, a palette, a way of setting type — so that seeing four of them together produces a fifth idea that none of them contains alone. That shared constraint is the part I find interesting. Freedom inside one rule, not freedom from all of them.

## Why it is on this site

The posters are not a detour from the engineering. Deciding what a screen should look like before writing it, knowing which element is the only important one, throwing away a version that is nearly right — that is the same work, on a different substrate.
