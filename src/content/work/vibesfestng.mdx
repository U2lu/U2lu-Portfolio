## The job

VibesfestNG, a party in Asaba, Delta State. One night, one date, a name people had to remember well enough to show up.

The deliverable was a campaign set rather than a single piece, because the same event had to announce itself in several places at once.

## Where it ran

Billboards and print, with the social set that travelled alongside them.

## One composition, three distances

Event artwork is read at three distances, and they pull in opposite directions.

At phone-thumbnail size it is glanced at, in a feed, for perhaps a fifth of a second. At A2 it is held and read. On a billboard it is moving past at speed. The same composition has to survive all three, which means the smallest version has to be designed first: if the name and the date do not land at thumbnail size, no amount of detail rescues the billboard.

## Print is a different medium

A screen adds light; ink subtracts it. A palette that separates cleanly on a monitor can collapse into mud in CMYK, and a rich dark wash on screen can go flat on stock. The colour decision has to survive the press, not just the artboard — which is the part of this work that screenshots never show.

## A set, not a poster

Four pieces carried the same night: the announcement, an artist reveal, a sold-out stamp, and a vendor confirmation card.

Seen together the system is obvious. The yellow field and the red-and-black type stay put; what changes is the hierarchy. The announcement leads with the lineup. The reveal leads with one face and one name. The sold-out version is the original flyer with a stamp across it, not a new artwork — which is the right call, because once the night is full the only new information is that it is full. And the vendor card leaves the centre of the frame free, so a different logo can drop into it once per vendor.

That last one is the tell. A vendor card gets made a dozen times. One visual language, four different jobs.
